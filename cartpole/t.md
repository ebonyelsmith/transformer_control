`Abstract
Balancing the cart-pole inverted pendulum traditionally demands explicit dynamics knowledge or extensive manual tuning of controllers. In response, we propose SAAM, a GPT-2 transformer-based, zero-shot controller leveraging in-context learning to stabilize unseen cart-pole configurations. Trained on 19.2 million simulated state-action trajectories generated by a composite swing-up and LQR controller, SAAM achieves stable control without gradient updates or explicit dynamics identification during inference. A novel Gaussian noise-injection technique prevents state magnitude vanishing, enabling effective learning of implicit LQR gains and seamless mode switching between swing-up and stabilization phases. Evaluation on previously unseen system parameters reveals significant generalization capabilities, with SAAM successfully stabilizing configurations outside the training distribution. Compared to baseline methods, SAAM demonstrates robustness, adaptability, and effective zero-shot generalization, achieving near-optimal control performance even with minimal context windows. Key technical innovations include noise-injected trajectory generation and periodic-aware input representations. Future work will extend SAAM’s approach to additional benchmark systems, such as Acrobot, exploring broader implications for adaptive transformer-based control.
Introduction
Control Theory and The Cart-Pole
The CartPole is a classic underactuated system consisting of a cart and a pivoted pole. The control goal is to swing the pole upright and balance it there. Traditionally, a two-stage controller is used: an energy-based swing-up controller first injects energy to get the pole near upright, then a Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) stabilizes it.
Tansformer Models and AI
The promise of modern machine learning is to overcome these issues by learning control policies directly from data, without requiring explicit system identification. Sequence models, particularly transformers, can in principle infer complex relationships and even adapt to new conditions on the fly (via in-context learning). Our research question is: Can a transformer learn the CartPole’s optimal control policy (swing-up + balance) purely from observing trajectories, and how can we ensure it learns the true LQR behavior? Our hypothesis was that by introducing the right training strategy – specifically, injecting noise to keep the control signal from vanishing – the transformer will implicitly learn the LQR control law and general control principles. We aimed to test whether such a model could not only imitate the training trajectories but also adapt to variations (e.g. different pole masses/lengths) and potentially extend to new systems, all without explicit knowledge of physics equations.
Method
The Transformer: Outputs a predicted control action at each time step, and internally it can also predict the mode (swing-up or balance) as a learned representation. We emphasize that the model is not provided any explicit equations or system parameters – it must infer the control policy from the data sequences alone.
Training Procedure: We generated an extensive dataset of 19 million expert controlled CartPole trajectories. Training was done on 3000 batches of 6400 sequence segments; failed trials are removed. In practice, using only ~25% of the collected data was sufficient to achieve convergence. The model was trained to minimize the error between its predicted actions and the expert controller’s actions using mean-squared error loss on the action only. The transformer quickly learned to reproduce the swing-up motion, but needed additional help learning the LQR controls and the switching behavior hence why we implimented noise injection and switching label respectively.
System and Data: We focus on the continuous-control CartPole environment. Importantly, we introduce a Gaussian noise injection in the LQR phase of each trajectory. Context-Dependent Performance
As expected the model’s performance depends on the length of context provided. With minimal context (e.g. Context 1, where the model only sees the very start state), the transformer tends to devise its own suboptimal stabilization strategy. In these cases, it underperforms the original controller – for instance, in the graph below the model swings 5 times before catching the pendulum with LQR. In contrast, with a longer context (e.g. Context 50), the model’s behavior outperforms or closely matches the ground-truth.

We observed a remarkable phenomenon: the model is able to significantly outperform the ground truth trajectories. Although trained solely on noisy LQR trajectories—where control actions never decayed to zero—our model achieved perfect zero-decay LQR behavior at inference. This emerged because it implicitly learned the feedback gain K; by the nature of LQR, this drives both state and control toward zero. Notably, we optimized only for action prediction, not for state error, yet the learned K naturally minimized the distance-to-goal metric.
